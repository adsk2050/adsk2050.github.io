Speech you can hear: TTS lessons from CS224S

What I learned about text-to-speech in Stanford's CS224S (Spoken Language Processing), one idea per slide. Each slide has a short explanation and a few clips that differ in one thing only. Play them one after the other and listen for the difference.

Arrow keys move between slides. Audio loads only when you press play.

Start here

How this works

Most of the clips come from Bulbul V4 Flash, Sarvam's text-to-speech model for Indian languages. In nine of the slides Bulbul also explains the idea out loud, in its own voice, before you hear the examples. Press Bulbul explains for that.

The streaming slide uses Kokoro, a small open model I ran on my laptop for an experiment.

Every clip is labelled A, B, C. Within a slide, only one thing changes between them: the script, the voice, the punctuation, the pace, or the channel. Each slide ends with the lecture and slides where CS224S teaches the idea.

Headphones help, especially on the phone-line slide.

Lesson 1 · Text normalisation

Numbers, dates and acronyms

Before a model can speak, it has to decide how to say what is written. ₹5,00,000 is "five lakh rupees", not "five comma zero zero". A date can be read several ways. A PNR is read digit by digit, a phone number in groups. Some acronyms are words (ISRO), others are letters (EMI).

This step is called text normalisation. It turns "non-standard words" into words you can pronounce, and it needs context to do it.

CS224S Lecture 3, slides 35 and 39–40: text normalisation of non-standard words.

Lesson 2 · Grapheme to phoneme

Devanagari vs romanised Hindi

A TTS model turns letters (graphemes) into sounds (phonemes). Devanagari spells Hindi sounds almost one to one. Romanised Hindi does not: "a" might be a short अ or a long आ, and the model has to guess.

Same sentence, same voice, two scripts.

Listen

CS224S Lecture 3, slides 41 and 45: letter-to-sound rules and G2P accuracy across languages.

Lesson 3 · Code-mixing

Hinglish: Hindi voice vs Hinglish voice

Indians mix Hindi and English inside one sentence. The usual way to write it for TTS is Hindi words in Devanagari and English words in Latin script. The front end then has to pick the right sounds for each word, in each language.

The same Hinglish sentence, first with a Hindi voice, then with a voice made for Hinglish. Listen to the English words. Bulbul's explanation here is itself in Hinglish.

Listen

CS224S Lecture 3, slides 23 and 45: the front end chooses the phones, and G2P depends on the language.

Lesson 4 · Prosody

Punctuation steers prosody

Prosody is the melody of speech: pitch, pauses, loudness and timing. Bulbul has no SSML and no pause tags, so punctuation is the only control you have. Prosody belongs to the phrase. Move a phrase boundary and the melody moves with it.

Same words, four kinds of mark: comma, full stop, exclamation, ellipsis.

Listen

CS224S Lecture 2, slide 49: prosodic boundaries. Lecture 3, slide 9: what prosody TTS must specify.

Lesson 5 · Duration

Speaking rate

Models like FastSpeech predict how long each sound lasts, then a "length regulator" repeats each sound's frames to fill that time. A pace setting just scales those predicted durations. Slow suits teaching, normal suits talking, fast suits announcements.

Listen

CS224S Lecture 5, slides 105–106: FastSpeech's length regulator and duration predictor.

Lesson 6 · Speaker and style

One speaker, four styles

Multi-speaker models learn a vector for each speaker (a speaker embedding). Style can be a second vector on top, as with Global Style Tokens. Bulbul's voice IDs show this split: simran_hi_news is speaker, language, style.

One speaker, Simran, in four styles. The words stay the same; only the delivery changes.

Listen

CS224S Lecture 5, slides 59 and 96: speaker embeddings and Global Style Tokens.

Lesson 7 · Languages

Ten languages, one hello

Each language has its own sounds and its own script, and a model is only as good as its data in each. The course shows how wide the gap is: letter-to-sound error is about 0.5% for English and Czech, but about 38% averaged over 507 languages.

One greeting in all ten languages that have Bulbul V4 voices. Hindi and English have over a hundred voices; Tamil, Telugu and Kannada have four each.

Listen

CS224S Lecture 3, slide 45: G2P error 0.535% for English and Czech vs 37.85% across 507 languages.

Lesson 8 · Telephony

On a phone line

Wideband speech is sampled at 16 kHz or more. A classic phone line samples at 8 kHz and passes only about 300 to 3400 Hz. The high frequencies that make "s" and "sh" crisp are cut off. The course notes that telephone speech roughly doubles speech-recognition error.

Most Indian voice bots live on this narrow channel, so a voice has to stay clear on it. Clip B is clip A, band-limited and resampled to 8 kHz the way a phone line would.

Listen

CS224S Lecture 4, slide 14: 16 kHz speech vs the 8 kHz telephone rate. Lecture 1, slide 29: telephone speech roughly doubles ASR error.

Experiment · Streaming TTS

Streaming: how small can a chunk be?

A voice agent wants to start speaking before the language model has finished writing. So it cuts the reply into chunks and sends each chunk to TTS as soon as it arrives. Smaller chunks mean the first sound comes sooner. I measured what else they change, with Kokoro-82M on a 67-second essay.

The surprise: chunks smaller than a clause make the audio itself longer. The duration predictor decides how long each sound lasts from the phrase around it. Give it a lone fragment and it stretches that fragment like a whole sentence. Token by token, the essay took 331 seconds to say instead of 67.

Lesson 9 · Limits

What TTS still cannot do

Modern TTS is natural but, as the course puts it, "not yet fully emotive". Bulbul V4 Flash, the fast model, has no laughter, whisper or emotion tags (those are promised for V4 Pro). It does not sing, cannot clone a voice, and has no SSML. Malayalam and Odia have no V4 voices yet.
