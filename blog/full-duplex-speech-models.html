Listening while talking: how full-duplex speech models work

Most voice agents take turns like a walkie-talkie: wait for silence, transcribe, think, speak. Full-duplex speech models listen and speak at the same time, and decide for themselves when to talk, when to stop and when to say “mm-hm”. This post covers the whole field, one idea per slide: the problem, the architectures, what goes in and out, how they are trained, and how they are judged.

The problem · Definitions

Half duplex and full duplex

The words come from telecoms. A half-duplex channel carries one direction at a time, like a walkie-talkie: you say “over” and wait. A full-duplex channel carries both directions at once, like a phone call.

Almost every voice agent in production is half-duplex inside, even when it runs on a full-duplex phone line. It waits for you to stop, then answers. A full-duplex speech model takes your audio in and puts its own audio out continuously, every few tens of milliseconds, whether anyone is talking or not. Silence, “mm-hm”, stopping mid-word and talking over each other are all just things it can output.

That matters because real conversation is not tidy turns:

  • Gaps are short. Across ten languages, the typical gap between one person stopping and the next starting is about 200 ms.
  • Overlap is normal. Listeners say “yeah”, “mm”, “haan” while the other person talks. In the Switchboard phone corpus about one utterance in five is a backchannel like this.
  • Pauses are ambiguous. A one-second silence can be the end of a turn, someone thinking, or someone talking to a person in the room.

The field has several names: full-duplex spoken dialogue models, full-duplex speech language models, or just duplex models. Older work on the same question is called turn-taking modelling.

Sources: Stivers et al., Universals and cultural variation in turn-taking, PNAS 2009; Jurafsky et al., Switchboard dialogue act tagging (SWBD-DAMSL); Chen and Yu, survey of full-duplex spoken language models, 2025.

The problem · The cascade

The cascade and where its time goes

The standard voice agent is a chain of four models: voice activity detection (VAD) finds speech, speech recognition (ASR) turns it into text, an LLM writes a reply, and text-to-speech (TTS) speaks it. It is modular, easy to debug and can use any text LLM. Its weak points are:

  • Latency adds up. Each stage waits for the one before.
  • Everything but the words is lost. Tone, hesitation, laughter, who is speaking and background sound do not survive the trip through text.
  • Turn-taking is a rule, not a skill. “Reply after N ms of silence” is either too eager or too slow.

Move the sliders to build a cascade’s budget, from the moment the caller stops to the first sound of the reply.

Production stacks fight this with tricks: a small learned turn detector instead of a fixed timer, starting the LLM before the turn is confirmed, streaming TTS in small chunks. These help, and they are why most shipped agents are still cascades. Full-duplex models attack the problem at the root: there is no pipeline to wait for.

Sources: Moshi (160 ms theoretical, 200 ms practical); Castillo-López et al. 2025 (deployed agents wait 700–1000 ms); Pipecat Smart Turn and LiveKit turn detector (learned end-of-turn).

Architecture · Tokenisers

Speech as tokens

A language model predicts the next token. To make it predict speech, speech has to become tokens. A neural audio codec does this: an encoder squeezes a short slice of audio into a vector, and residual vector quantisation (RVQ) turns that vector into a few numbers from fixed codebooks. The first codebook captures the rough shape, and each next one encodes what the previous ones missed.

There are two kinds of token. Semantic tokens keep what is said and drop who said it. Acoustic tokens keep everything needed to rebuild the sound. Duplex models need both: semantic tokens for the language model to reason over, acoustic tokens to sound like a person. SpeechTokenizer shows the split directly: its first layer alone keeps the words but loses the speaker; all eight keep both.

Why 12.5 Hz matters. Fewer frames per second means shorter sequences, so a 7B model can run a step every 80 ms and hold minutes of conversation in context. Semantic-only tokens need a separate decoder to become sound, usually flow matching to a spectrogram and then a HiFi-GAN vocoder. Mimi has its own decoder.

Architecture · The big split

Two families of design

The first survey of the field sorts models into three classes: half-duplex (strict turns), pseudo-full-duplex (switches between listening and speaking very fast) and true full-duplex (listens and speaks in the same step). It then splits the duplex ones by where the decision to speak lives.

  • Engineered synchronisation. Keep a turn-based speech LLM and add a small controller that decides, every chunk, whether to listen, speak or stop. Freeze-Omni’s head predicts three states per chunk: keep listening, the user is done so answer, or the user is done but do not interrupt. MinMo’s duplex predictor is one transformer layer on the LLM’s hidden states. VITA runs two copies of the model: one answers, one listens. The upside: the text LLM keeps its knowledge.
  • Learned synchronisation. One model sees both streams and outputs its own stream every frame. There is no “turn” anywhere in the code. Silence is just a run of silence tokens. Timing, overlap and backchannels come from the training data.

Many models that sound duplex in demos are half-duplex in their papers: Qwen2.5-Omni, Qwen3-Omni, GLM-4-Voice, Mini-Omni and LLaMA-Omni all take turns. They are often the starting point that duplex work converts.

Sources: Chen and Yu, survey; Freeze-Omni; MinMo; VITA.

Architecture · The reference design

Moshi, piece by piece

Moshi (Kyutai, September 2024) is the open model most duplex work builds on. Every 80 ms it takes one frame of the caller’s audio and produces one frame of its own.

  • Mimi turns 24 kHz audio into 8 tokens per 80 ms frame, and back.
  • 17 streams per frame: 1 text token, 8 audio tokens for Moshi, 8 for the caller.
  • RQ-Transformer. Predicting 17 × thousands of steps with one big model would be too slow. So a big Temporal Transformer (7B, starting from the Helium text LLM) moves through time, one step per frame. A small Depth Transformer (6 layers) then fills in the tokens inside that frame, one after another.
  • Inner monologue. Before its audio tokens, Moshi predicts a text token for the same moment: the word it is about to say. This lets the text LLM’s knowledge steer the speech. Text is sparser than audio, so about 65% of text slots are a PAD token.
  • Acoustic delay. The acoustic tokens trail the semantic token by one frame, so the model knows what it is saying before it decides how it sounds.

Latency: one 80 ms frame plus the 80 ms acoustic delay gives 160 ms in theory. Kyutai reports about 200 ms in practice.

Source: Défossez et al., Moshi: a speech-text foundation model for real-time dialogue, 2024; code and weights.

Architecture · Simulation

Inside Moshi, frame by frame

Each column is one 80 ms frame. Step through it. At every frame the model writes the top three rows (its text, its semantic token, its acoustic tokens). The bottom two rows are the caller, coming in from the microphone. Here Moshi says “Hi there, how can I help” while the caller says a short “hello?” over it.

· is a PAD text token. “sil” is a codec token for silence: silence is coded like any other sound. “×7” stands for the seven acoustic tokens.

Notice three things. Moshi’s text runs slightly ahead of its audio. Its acoustic row lags its semantic row by one frame. And the caller’s rows are never empty: Moshi hears the caller even while it talks, which is what lets it stop, carry on or backchannel.

Architecture · Variants

Other ways to mix two speakers

The core question is how to fit two people talking at once into a model that reads one sequence.

ModelHow it handles two speakers
dGSLM (2022)Two transformer towers with shared weights, one per channel, joined by cross-attention. No text at all, so the timing was natural and the words were babble.
SyncLLM (2024)Llama 3 8B with HuBERT tokens. Speakers alternate in 160–240 ms chunks, each with a speaker tag that works as a clock. It predicts the caller’s next chunk too, which hides up to 240 ms of network lag.
OmniFlatten (2024)Cuts all streams into small chunks and flattens them into one sequence for an unchanged 0.5B GPT. Trained in stages, dropping the text streams one by one.
LSLM (2024)Adds a listening channel to a speaking model. Tested merging it at the input, in every layer, or at the output; merging in every layer worked best.
SALMONN-omni (2024)No audio tokens at all: continuous embeddings in and out, with explicit <start_speak> and <end_speak> tokens. Hears its own voice, so it handles echo.
PersonaPlex (2026)Moshi’s design, plus a prompt: a voice sample on the agent’s audio channel and a role description on its text channel.

Sources: dGSLM; SyncLLM; OmniFlatten; LSLM; SALMONN-omni; PersonaPlex.

Inputs and outputs

What goes in, what comes out

  • The caller’s audio streams in without a break, silence included.
  • Its own output goes back in, so the model knows what it has said and whether it is mid-sentence.
  • Prompts. PersonaPlex puts a few seconds of a voice on the agent’s audio channel (to clone it) and a role description on the text channel, before the call starts. Omni models like Qwen3-Omni also take images and video.
  • Outputs are audio tokens for the decoder, text tokens (the inner monologue, mostly padding), and in engineered designs, state tokens. 2026 models add tool calls as a separate output stream.

Training · Stages

The training recipe

Real two-person conversation with each voice on its own channel is rare. So training starts from cheap, plentiful data and moves step by step toward the scarce data that teaches timing.

  1. Text LM. Moshi starts from Helium, a 7B text model trained on 2.1 trillion tokens. This is where its knowledge comes from.
  2. Audio pretraining. About 7 million hours of speech, transcribed by Whisper to make the aligned text stream. One stream only: the model learns to talk, not to converse.
  3. Simulated two streams. Speakers are separated by diarisation. One, picked at random, becomes “Moshi”; everyone else becomes “the caller”.
  4. Real calls. Fisher: 2,000 hours of phone calls, each side on its own channel. Here the model learns real gaps, overlaps and backchannels. The 8 kHz phone audio is upsampled to 24 kHz first.
  5. Instructions. Over 20,000 hours of synthetic dialogue: Helium writes the conversations, a two-speaker TTS voices them with one consistent voice for Moshi, and noise and reverb are added to the caller’s side.
  6. Timing rewards (2026, later papers). Reinforcement learning on when to speak, wait, backchannel or yield. See the slide after next.

Other models follow the same shape. SyncLLM goes from turn-based synthetic speech, to duplex format with no overlap, to Fisher. OmniFlatten goes from speech recognition and TTS, to half-duplex, to full-duplex, removing the text streams one at a time.

Sources: Moshi, section 4; SyncLLM; OmniFlatten.

Training · Data

Where the conversation data comes from

Hours of audio in each source, on a log scale. Real two-channel conversation (orange) is a thin slice of what models train on.

Three ways to make two-channel data when you only have one:

  • Diarise and split. Find who speaks when, and put each speaker on their own track (Moshi’s stage 3).
  • Voice a script. Have an LLM write the dialogue, voice each turn with TTS, and place the turns on two tracks with chosen gaps. A negative gap makes an overlap or a barge-in (SyncLLM, OmniFlatten, PersonaPlex).
  • Separate real recordings. Pull each speaker out of podcasts with source separation (DuplexChat, about 415,000 hours of English and Japanese).

Synthetic data is cheap and controllable, but it misses what makes real talk real: people matching each other’s pace and pitch, long overlaps, and other voices in the room. The 2026 survey of the field calls open, real training data its main frontier. For Indian languages it is thinner still: Human-1, the first open Hindi duplex model, trained on 26,000 hours of real two-channel conversation collected for it.

Sources: Switchboard and Fisher (LDC); Seamless Interaction; Human-1; SyncLLM; DuplexChat; MinMo; 2026 survey.

Training · Reinforcement learning

Teaching timing with rewards

Supervised training on conversation copies the average behaviour in the data. The result is often too much silence, or turns taken at the wrong moment. In 2026, several groups started to train timing directly with reinforcement learning.

The first lesson was that naive RL breaks things. Rewarding the whole token stream for good timing damaged what the model said: it started repeating itself. The fix, shared by most 2026 papers, is to separate “when to speak” from “what to say” and reward only the timing decision.

WorkDateIdea
ASPIRinApr 2026Collapse the output to one choice, speak or stay silent, and run GRPO on that choice only. Better timing, half the repetition of plain GRPO.
Interactivity alignmentJun 2026Separate rewards for pause handling, turn-taking, backchannels and interruptions, on short windows cut from human conversation. Applied to Moshi and PersonaPlex.
BayLing-DuplexJun 2026Supervised training on 400k duplex samples, then preference optimisation (DPO) for turn and barge-in timing.
DuplexPOJul 2026A reward split into start, backchannel, yield and participation. Keeps the base model’s reasoning.
HiPLEXOct 2026Two policies: a control policy (pad, end pad, speak) and a content policy. Timing reward goes only to control.

Smaller tricks that protect the content. Moshi down-weights the loss on acoustic tokens and on padding text tokens, so most of the learning goes into the words. Freeze-Omni keeps its text LLM frozen for the whole of training. GLM-4-Voice keeps text a few tokens ahead of speech so the words are decided first.

Also: Dual-Axis Generative Reward Model; SteerablePlex; Freeze-Omni; GLM-4-Voice.

Judging · Overview

Five things to measure

A text chatbot is judged mostly on what it says. A duplex speech model has to be judged on five things, and they pull against each other.

AxisQuestionTypical measures
TimingDoes it speak and stop at the right moments?Gaps, pauses, overlaps; takeover rate; agreement with a turn-taking judge
InteractionDoes it handle backchannels, interruptions, side talk, corrections?Full-Duplex-Bench and its relatives; behaviour labels (respond, resume)
LatencyHow fast does it answer, and how fast does it stop?Response latency, stop latency, time to first audio

Every benchmark also defines latency its own way, so latency numbers from different benchmarks cannot be compared.

Judging · Conversation statistics

Timing: IPUs, pauses, gaps and overlaps

The oldest way to judge timing comes from conversation analysis. Run voice activity detection on each speaker’s channel and cut it into pieces:

  • IPU (inter-pausal unit): a stretch of speech with at least 200 ms of silence on each side.
  • Pause: silence between two IPUs of the same speaker.
  • Gap: silence between IPUs of different speakers. A negative gap is an overlap. Together they are the floor-transfer offset.
  • Overlap: time when both speak.

A good model’s totals should look like real conversation. Moshi’s paper continues 10-second Fisher phone-call prompts and adds up each one:

SystemIPUPauseGapOverlapNote

Human reference values. In ten languages, answers to yes/no questions start a mean 208 ms after the question ends; the most common offset is 0 ms. A single word takes about 600 ms to plan, so people plan their reply while the other person is still talking. In Switchboard, overlap is about 4% of the audio, and about 30% of turn changes happen in overlap.

Judging · Behaviour tests

Full-Duplex-Bench: the reference test

Statistics say whether a model’s timing looks human on average. Full-Duplex-Bench (2025) asks whether it does the right thing in four specific moments. Its key measure is the takeover rate (TOR): the share of moments where the model takes the turn. Saying anything other than silence or a short backchannel (under 1 s and under two words) counts as a takeover.

Judging · Later versions

Overlap, multi-turn and tools

Each new version of Full-Duplex-Bench adds a harder part of real calls.

v1.5: what to do when someone talks over you

Four overlap cases: the user interrupts (should respond), the user backchannels, the user talks to someone else, and background speech (all three: should carry on). It adds stop latency (from the start of the overlap to the model going quiet) and response latency, and GPT-4o labels each reply as respond or resume. GPT-4o Realtime stops fastest, in 0.23 s, but almost never resumes when the user talks to someone else (2%) or there is background speech (4%). Systems turn out to be either responsive or floor-holding.

v2: many turns with a simulated caller

gpt-realtime plays a caller with goals, at fast or slow pace. A judge LLM scores the transcript 1–5 on turn-taking, instruction following, corrections (“no, I meant Tuesday”), entity tracking and safety. Fast pace, corrections: GPT-Realtime 4.0, Moshi 2.9, Freeze-Omni 2.7.

v3: tool calls with real, messy speech

100 scenarios with five kinds of disfluency, such as self-corrections. Pass@1 means every expected tool was called with every argument correct. GPT-Realtime is best at 0.60. A plain cascade takes a turn every time but needs 10 s per task.

Sources: v1.5, v2, v3.

Judging · Learned judges

A judge trained on people

Fixed rules (“a backchannel is under 1 s”) are crude. Talking Turns (ICLR 2025) instead trains a turn-taking model on the Switchboard phone corpus. Every 40 ms it looks at the last 30 s of audio and predicts what a person would do next: stay silent, backchannel, interrupt, take the turn, or keep talking. It scores about 0.91 ROC-AUC on held-out human conversation.

That model then becomes the judge. At five kinds of moment, it checks whether the system did what a person would likely have done:

  1. The user pauses: should the system speak?
  2. The user is speaking: should it backchannel?
  3. The user is speaking: should it interrupt?
  4. The system pauses: does it signal whether the user may speak?
  5. The user interrupts: does the system yield?

Findings: systems often misjudge when to speak, interrupt too much, and almost never backchannel. Moshi rarely yields when interrupted.

The newest benchmark in this style, TurnBench (2026), uses 30 hours of hand-labelled conversation. Its sharpest finding: people start talking a median 151 ms before the other person finishes. No system matches that without too many false starts.

Sources: Talking Turns; TurnBench.

Judging · Content

Is it still smart?

Adding speech to an LLM usually costs it some intelligence. Moshi on spoken questions, compared with its own text backbone answering the same questions as text:

Spoken QA setMoshi (speech)Helium (text)
Web Questions26.632.3
Llama Questions62.375.0
TriviaQA (audio)22.856.4

An answer counts as right if the gold answer appears in a transcript of the model’s spoken reply.

Broader suites:

  • VoiceBench: spoken versions of AlpacaEval, IFEval, OpenBookQA, MMSU, accented QA and refusal tests, plus robustness to speaker, noise and disfluency. Whisper + GPT-4o scores 87.2, GPT-4o audio 86.4, Moshi 27.5.
  • URO-Bench: speech in, speech out; understanding, reasoning and oral conversation, in English, Chinese and seven more languages. A cascade beats GLM-4-Voice on all three (reasoning 82.9 vs 55.5).
  • Big Bench Audio: 1,000 Big Bench Hard reasoning questions, read aloud.
  • SD-Eval: does the reply suit the speaker’s emotion, accent, age and surroundings?
  • MMAU and AIR-Bench: understanding of speech, sounds and music.

This is why 2026 methods such as DuplexPO and D2 put so much effort into keeping the base model’s ability while teaching timing.

Judging · Caveats

Who judges the judges?

  • Transcript judges lose the sound. Most benchmarks transcribe the audio and ask a text LLM to score it. Timing, tone and hesitation disappear, and transcription errors leak into the score.
  • Audio judges have biases. Audio LLMs used as judges can agree well with human preference (0.91 Spearman in AudioJudge), but prefer longer answers and the first clip they hear.
  • Static benchmarks miss what users want. In Talk Arena, 20 standard benchmarks explained only about 30% of what real users preferred in 7,500 conversations.
  • MOS is not portable. A review of 133 TTS papers found MOS shifts with how the test is run.
  • Numbers are self-reported. Most results here are the authors’ own, on their own set-up, and latency depends on hardware and network.

The test that matters is still a real conversation. Benchmarks are useful for finding specific failures, and weak at ranking systems overall.

Sources: AudioJudge; Talk Arena; Kirkland et al. 2023.

Where it stands · Agents

Fast talker, slow thinker

A duplex model has to emit a frame every 80 ms. That leaves no time for long reasoning or a tool call that takes two seconds. The 2026 answer is to split the work, much like the “system 1, system 2” idea from psychology:

  • A fast duplex speech model owns the conversation: timing, backchannels, interruptions, small talk, “let me check that”.
  • A slow text LLM or agent behind it does reasoning, tool calls and memory, and hands results back.

The link between them varies. NVIDIA’s frontend-backend design has the speech model emit a delegate token and stream the transcript to a text LLM, then inject the result back; it reports 92–97% tool-call recall with turn-taking intact. SALMONN-duo learns when to answer itself and when to delegate. NemotronLabs VoiceChat and DuplexSLA go the other way and put tool calls on their own output stream inside one model.

Related ideas: thinking while listening (reasoning during the user’s turn, so it costs no extra time) and retrieval inside the frame budget (feeding retrieved text into a running model).

Sources: frontend-backend tool calls; SALMONN-duo; NemotronLabs VoiceChat; DuplexSLA; Chronological Thinking; SHANKS.

One more thing worth knowing: production systems from OpenAI, Google and Amazon still expose a separate, tunable turn-detection setting. Fully learned turn-taking leads in research, and lags in robustness on long, noisy, many-speaker calls.

What to read first

  1. Chen and Yu, From Turn-Taking to Synchronous Dialogue (2025). A short, clear map of designs and evaluation.
  2. A Survey of Full-Duplex Spoken Dialogue Systems (2026). The newest survey, with a vocabulary for where decisions are made.
  3. The Moshi paper (2024). The design most open work builds on, and the clearest case against cascades.
  4. Full-Duplex-Bench v1 and v1.5. The behaviours, in measurable terms.
  5. Talking Turns (ICLR 2025). How to judge timing with a model trained on human conversation.
  6. WavChat and On the Landscape of Spoken Language Models. Tokenisers, training and evaluation for speech LMs in general.
  7. IndicFDB and Human-1. The India-specific work: a ten-language benchmark and a Hindi duplex model.

The field moves fast. Over a hundred arXiv papers had “full-duplex” in the title in 2026 alone, so treat every number here as a snapshot from November 2026.
